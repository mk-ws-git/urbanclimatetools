## Background

Santiago sits in a semi-arid basin ringed by the Andes, where summer heat and poor ventilation concentrate high temperatures — and green space is distributed deeply unequally. Wealthier eastern communes have abundant tree canopy and parks; dense pericentral and southern communes have little, so the urban heat island falls hardest on the residents least able to cope with it. Heat adaptation in Santiago is therefore also an equity question.

## The intervention

Research groups and the regional government have pushed **nature-based cooling targeted at the most heat-exposed, least-green areas**. The **Stgo+ green-infrastructure plan**, piloted from 2018 by the University of Chile with the regional housing authority, sets out **10 strategies and 37 actions** across scales — from converting vacant lots into parks, to green roofs, to rainwater-managing public squares. University studies use land-surface-temperature analysis and microclimate simulation to identify where new green space would cut heat the most.

## Results

- Studies of southern pericentral communes find **urban parks cool their surroundings by about 1°C**, with the effect strongest under dense, tall tree canopy.
- Simulation work indicates green roofs can meaningfully lower neighbourhood air temperatures in Santiago's climate.
- The Stgo+ framework provides a prioritisation method — matching interventions to the communes with the highest heat vulnerability and lowest canopy.

## Lessons

Santiago's value here is the **method**: using heat and canopy mapping to direct limited green-infrastructure budgets to where they close the biggest equity gap, rather than where greening is easiest. The caveat is that much of the evidence is still pilot- and simulation-based — a formal citywide evaluation of measured canopy gain and temperature change is the missing piece, and claims should stay at the study scale until that exists.
