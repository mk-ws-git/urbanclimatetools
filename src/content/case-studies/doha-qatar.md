## Background

Doha is one of the hottest cities on Earth, with summer temperatures regularly above 45°C and high humidity off the Gulf. Qatar's response has leaned heavily on mechanical cooling — air conditioning accounts for roughly **80% of the country's electricity use** — which cools indoor (and increasingly outdoor) space at the cost of energy, emissions and waste heat, a loop that worsens the underlying problem. The adaptation question for Doha is how to keep people safe in extreme heat without only adding more compressors.

## The intervention

Two tracks run in parallel. First, **engineered outdoor cooling and shading**: the public works authority Ashghal has built shaded, actively cooled walking and jogging routes in city parks (for example at Al Gharrafa and Umm Al Seneem), and large districts rely on **district cooling** rather than building-by-building units. Second, **evidence for nature-based alternatives**: researchers at Hamad Bin Khalifa University (HBKU) use ENVI-met and building-energy simulation to test how far greening could substitute for mechanical cooling in Doha's climate.

## Results

- HBKU modelling of a combined strategy — intensive green roofs and façades, street trees and an urban park — projects **more than 4°C lower peak air temperature**, shorter extreme-heat-stress periods, and **over 20% (up to ~24%) lower building cooling demand**.
- Ashghal's cooled, shaded park routes make outdoor activity viable through peak summer for residents who would otherwise be confined indoors.

## Lessons

Doha is the Gulf test of an uncomfortable trade-off: mechanical and district cooling deliver reliable relief today but entrench energy demand and waste heat, while the nature-based gains are so far **modelled rather than measured** on the ground. The honest read is that extreme-heat adaptation here is real but early — the modelling sets a target (significant cooling and energy savings are physically achievable), and the next step is built pilots that verify those numbers outdoors.
