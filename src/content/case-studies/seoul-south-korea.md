## Background

For decades the Cheonggyecheon stream in central Seoul was buried beneath an elevated urban expressway carrying some 168,000 vehicles a day. The structure was ageing and the district was hot, polluted and congested — a textbook case of car-era infrastructure sitting on top of a natural watercourse.

## The intervention

Beginning in 2003, the city demolished the expressway and restored a **5.8 km stream** through downtown Seoul, reopening it as a linear public park in 2005. The project combined flood conveyance, a continuous water flow (pumped and supplemented), riparian planting and pedestrian space. It was politically bold — removing a major road rather than widening it — and became an international reference for "freeway removal" and blue-green corridors.

## Results

- **Local cooling:** air temperatures along the restored corridor measured **3.3–5.9°C cooler** than a parallel road a few blocks away; satellite analysis has also found substantially lower land-surface temperatures along the stream.
- **Biodiversity:** the number of recorded plant species rose from **62 to 308** between 2003 and 2008 — about a **639% increase** in overall biodiversity, alongside returning fish, birds and insects.
- **Flood capacity:** the restored channel was engineered to carry a 200-year storm, improving downtown flood conveyance.
- The corridor draws tens of thousands of visitors daily and catalysed surrounding regeneration.

## Lessons

Cheonggyecheon shows that removing car infrastructure can deliver measurable cooling, biodiversity and amenity gains at once — but it is not "free nature": the stream relies on supplemented flow, and critics note the restored channel is an engineered park rather than a fully natural river. The decisive ingredient was sustained political will to take road space back.
