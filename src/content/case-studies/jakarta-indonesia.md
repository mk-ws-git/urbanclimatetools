## Background

Jakarta sits on a low, river-crossed coastal plain. Indonesia's national research agency BRIN estimates the land is sinking by about 3.5 centimetres a year on average, and parts of the city have dropped by up to 4 metres over the past four decades. Other estimates give wider ranges by neighbourhood, so treat any single figure as indicative.

## The cause

Experts identify massive groundwater extraction by households, offices and industry as the main driver of subsidence. Jakarta's piped-water network reaches only a little over a quarter of households, so many people and businesses rely on pumped groundwater. Rules restricting extraction have covered limited areas and enforcement has been criticised as lax. Some consultants argue that stopping deep groundwater use would halt the sinking within years.

## The response

- **Giant sea wall (Garuda / NCICD):** the original concept combined a roughly 15-mile outer sea wall with 17 artificial islands to close off Jakarta Bay. Critics say it does not address subsidence, the underlying reason for flooding
- **Scaled up:** the president has since pushed a much larger plan for a sea wall along Java's northern coast, with an initial stage on about 21 km of Jakarta's north coast; wider cost estimates run as high as $60 billion
- **Water supply:** the city has said that by 2030 piped water will reach the whole city, which would reduce the need for groundwater

Current construction status was not confirmed in the sources used for this entry.

## Lessons

Flood protection that ignores the cause of the sinking offers limited protection, as BRIN researchers have noted. Closing the piped-water gap and enforcing groundwater limits are the measures most directly tied to slowing subsidence.
