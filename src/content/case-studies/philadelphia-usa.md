## Background

Like many older US cities, Philadelphia has a combined sewer system: stormwater and sewage share the same pipes, so heavy rain triggers combined-sewer overflows (CSOs) that discharge untreated flows into the Schuylkill and Delaware rivers. The conventional fix — vastly larger storage tunnels — would have cost far more and done nothing else for the city.

## The intervention

In 2011 Philadelphia Water entered a 25-year agreement, **Green City, Clean Waters**, choosing **green stormwater infrastructure** — rain gardens, bioswales, tree trenches, permeable paving and green roofs — as the primary tool to keep rain out of the sewers in the first place. Progress is measured in **"greened acres"** (an acre of impervious surface whose first inch of runoff is managed), with binding five-year milestones rising to roughly **10,000 greened acres** and an **85% reduction in CSO volume by 2036**. Total investment is estimated at about **US$2.4 billion** over 25 years.

## Results

- The city **surpassed its first five-year (2016) targets** for runoff and overflow reduction.
- Thousands of distributed green infrastructure installations across streets, schools and parks, maintained as a citywide asset network.
- Co-benefits beyond water: cooler, greener streets, habitat and neighbourhood amenity — the reason green was chosen over a single large grey tunnel.

## Lessons

Philadelphia is the reference case that distributed green infrastructure can be a primary stormwater strategy at city scale, not just a demonstration. The trade-off is operational: thousands of small assets must be inspected and maintained for decades, and verifying "greened acres" against overflow reduction requires sustained monitoring. Published acreage should be checked against the latest annual report rather than the original targets.
