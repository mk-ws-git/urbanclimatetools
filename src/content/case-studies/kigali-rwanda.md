## Background

Kigali is built across steep hills and valley-floor wetlands. Rapid growth pushed farming, dumping and informal settlement into those wetlands, degrading the natural sponges that once absorbed the city's heavy rains — and flooding grew more frequent and deadly as a result. Rwanda's response treats wetlands not as wasteland to be built on but as infrastructure to be protected and rebuilt.

## The intervention

The flagship is **Nyandungu Eco-Park**: over about three years a degraded valley swamp was converted into a **121-hectare** restored wetland with ponds, a riverine forest, walking trails and recreation areas, opened in 2022 at a cost of roughly **US$5 million** (World Bank, GEF and Rwanda Green Fund support). It is the demonstration model for a wider programme under the **Kigali Master Plan 2050**, which designates valley wetlands for restoration and targets on the order of **18,000 acres** citywide. The largest and most degraded site, **Gikondo**, entered its rehabilitation phase in 2024.

## Results

- **121 ha** of wetland restored at Nyandungu, now functioning as stormwater catchment, pollution filter and public green space.
- A pipeline of further sites under the 2050 plan, with Gikondo's ~US$27 million phase underway.
- Land-cover analysis of Gikondo found wetland area began recovering after 2010, consistent with early restoration progress.

## Lessons

Kigali reframes wetlands as flood infrastructure — cheaper and more multifunctional than hard drainage. The hard part is social, not technical: restoration displaces farming and informal livelihoods (the 2050 plan could affect more than 14,000 farming households), so a credible livelihood-transition plan is as important as the engineering. Outcomes at the newer sites like Gikondo are still being realised and should be tracked against completion, not announcement.
